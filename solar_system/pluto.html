Pluto

Pluto is a dwarf planet located in the Kuiper Belt, a region of icy bodies beyond Neptune. It was considered the ninth planet in the solar system until it was reclassified as a dwarf planet in 2006.

Discovery and Reclassification

Pluto was discovered in 1930 by astronomer Clyde Tombaugh and was originally classified as the ninth planet. In 2006, the International Astronomical Union redefined the criteria for what constitutes a planet, and Pluto was reclassified as a dwarf planet because it had not cleared its orbital neighborhood of other debris.

Size and Distance

Pluto has a diameter of about 2,377 kilometers, making it smaller than Earth's Moon. It orbits the Sun at an average distance of about 5.9 billion kilometers, though its distance varies significantly due to its elliptical orbit.

Orbit

Pluto has a highly elliptical and tilted orbit compared to the planets, which sometimes brings it closer to the Sun than Neptune. It takes about 248 Earth years to complete a single orbit around the Sun.

Composition and Atmosphere

Pluto is composed of rock and ice, with a surface covered in frozen nitrogen, methane, and carbon monoxide. It has a thin atmosphere that expands and contracts as it moves closer to and farther from the Sun, thickening slightly when it is nearer to the Sun.

Moons

Pluto has five known moons, the largest of which, Charon, is about half the size of Pluto itself. The Pluto-Charon system is sometimes considered a double dwarf planet due to their similar sizes, as they orbit a shared center of gravity located between them.

Exploration

Pluto was visited by NASA's New Horizons spacecraft in 2015, which provided the first close-up images and detailed data about its surface, atmosphere, and moons. The mission revealed a surprisingly complex and geologically active world, including mountains made of water ice and a large heart-shaped plain of nitrogen ice.
